Where we disagree with the bookmaker is where this model is worth reading. On the tenth of players we differ from the book on most across 2023-2025, our estimate landed 28% closer to what actually happened.

Read that in two halves. Where we rate a player lower than the book we are 54% closer — our sharpest skill, and not something these markets let you bet. Where we rate one higher we are 17% closer, and that is the half you can back.

What does “closer” mean here?

Both our estimate and the book's are probabilities, and the player either scored or did not. Square the miss for each, average over the players, and compare. “Closer” is the percentage by which our average squared miss is smaller than the book's.

  • 0% would mean we match the book. Anything above zero means we beat it, so both halves above are wins — 54% is simply a much bigger one than 17%.
  • It is not a win rate. There is no 50% baseline here, and it does not count how often we were nearer — one badly wrong call costs more than two near-misses, which is the point of squaring.
  • Each half is measured only on the players where we disagree with the book in that direction, and only on the tenth of players we disagree with most. Agreement is not skill, so it is excluded.

The gap between the two halves is the honest story: we are far better at spotting who will not score than who will. That is the more useful skill and the one you cannot bet, because these books do not post a “no” price on touchdown props.

How to read this — and how the number is built
Reading the table

Our estimate is our answer: how likely this player is to score. Bookmaker says is theirs, with their profit margin stripped out so the two are directly comparable. Difference is the gap between them, and Our read turns that gap into a verdict. Typical at # is what a player in that ranking slot normally does, so you can see whether this one is unusual for the slot.

Two different questions live here. Will it happen? is Our estimate — a player at 65% is genuinely likely to score, whatever the price says. Is the price wrong? is Difference — a player at 15% priced as 10% is undervalued, and will still fail to score 85% of the time. A big Difference is not a prediction that something surprising happens; it is a claim the price is off.

How the number is built

1. Learn from 9 seasons. Every play from 2015 through 2025 — carries, catches, targets, snaps, injury reports, depth charts, betting lines. The model studies seasons through 2023 and is then tested on ones it has never seen.

2. Score every player. We compute about 319 numbers per player per game. What matters most isn't total yards, it's where the work happens — carries inside the 5-yard line, red-zone targets, opening-drive touches — plus the offence around him, the specific defence he faces, the point total and spread, weather, rest, and who's injured.

> Every one of those must have been knowable before kickoff. That is the rule > everything rests on, and automated tests check it.

3. Split the game in two. Rather than asking each player an isolated yes/no, we ask two questions the game actually answers: how many touchdowns will this team score — read from the betting lines, historically 2.43 per team per game — and whose will they be, from usage and matchup. Multiply the two. Teammates then compete for the same touchdowns instead of being counted independently, and a game's numbers add up on their own rather than being rescaled afterwards.

4. Weigh the bookmaker in. We neither override them nor copy them. A weight fitted from past results combines both, refitted each week using only games that had already finished, so it can never peek forward.

How well it works

Scored across 1,130 games in 2022-2025 — each season predicted by a model retrained without it — our top-rated player scored 51% of the time (give or take 1%), and at least one of our top five scored 95%.

Against the sportsbook on the 20,957 anytime touchdown prices we both quoted across 2023-2025, our estimates were 8.3% more accurate overall, and 27.9% more accurate on the tenth of players we disagree with them on most.

What this is not. It is a well-calibrated usage-and-matchup model that agrees with the bookmaker most of the time and beats them modestly at sorting players. It is not a money printer, and everything above describes a replay at closing prices — no bet in it was ever actually struck.

Where the accuracy comes from. Most of our advantage on big disagreements is in correctly rating players down, which these markets give you no way to back. The half you can bet is a real but smaller edge, and it has been shrinking season over season. Betting reality has the measured version of all of this.

How the model has performed on 2025 — a season it never trained on. Training stops at 2023, 2024 is used for tuning, and 2025 is held back entirely so these numbers aren't flattering themselves.

How to read this — and how the number is built
Reading the table

Our estimate is our answer: how likely this player is to score. Bookmaker says is theirs, with their profit margin stripped out so the two are directly comparable. Difference is the gap between them, and Our read turns that gap into a verdict. Typical at # is what a player in that ranking slot normally does, so you can see whether this one is unusual for the slot.

Two different questions live here. Will it happen? is Our estimate — a player at 65% is genuinely likely to score, whatever the price says. Is the price wrong? is Difference — a player at 15% priced as 10% is undervalued, and will still fail to score 85% of the time. A big Difference is not a prediction that something surprising happens; it is a claim the price is off.

How the number is built

1. Learn from 9 seasons. Every play from 2015 through 2025 — carries, catches, targets, snaps, injury reports, depth charts, betting lines. The model studies seasons through 2023 and is then tested on ones it has never seen.

2. Score every player. We compute about 320 numbers per player per game. What matters most isn't total yards, it's where the work happens — carries inside the 5-yard line, red-zone targets, opening-drive touches — plus the offence around him, the specific defence he faces, the point total and spread, weather, rest, and who's injured.

> Every one of those must have been knowable before kickoff. That is the rule > everything rests on, and automated tests check it.

3. Share out one touchdown. Exactly one player scores first, so each player's number is his share of that single event and a game's numbers add to 100%.

4. Weigh the bookmaker in. We neither override them nor copy them. A weight fitted from past results combines both, refitted each week using only games that had already finished, so it can never peek forward.

How well it works

Scored across 1,115 games in 2022-2025 — each season predicted by a model retrained without it — our top-rated player scored 16% of the time (give or take 1%), and at least one of our top five scored 53%.

Against the sportsbook on the 20,620 first touchdown prices we both quoted across 2023-2025, our estimates were 2.5% more accurate overall, and 7.7% more accurate on the tenth of players we disagree with them on most.

What this is not. It is a well-calibrated usage-and-matchup model that agrees with the bookmaker most of the time and beats them modestly at sorting players. It is not a money printer, and everything above describes a replay at closing prices — no bet in it was ever actually struck.

Where the accuracy comes from. Most of our advantage on big disagreements is in correctly rating players down, which these markets give you no way to back. The half you can bet is a real but smaller edge, and it has been shrinking season over season. Betting reality has the measured version of all of this.

How the model has performed on 2025 — a season it never trained on. Training stops at 2023, 2024 is used for tuning, and 2025 is held back entirely so these numbers aren't flattering themselves.
